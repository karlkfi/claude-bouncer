# An indented citation fragment is never checked, at any window

`queue.py lint`'s `stale-citation` check is documented as tolerant — a fragment
within `CITATION_WINDOW` lines of its number is a citation that still works, and
`--citation-window 0` is the exact gate for a caller that wants certainty. Both
readings assume the fragment is being compared at all. Where the fragment opens
with whitespace it is not, at any window and at any distance, because
`CITATION_RE` requires a non-space first character:

`scripts/queue.py:60:CITATION_RE = re.compile(`

The third group is ``(?::(\S[^`\n]*)(?=`))?`` and it is optional, so a leading
space fails the group rather than the match. `fragment` comes back `None`, and
`scripts/queue.py:643:if fragment is None:` skips every check below it. What
survives is the file-length check — the one way a citation rarely fails.

Measured 2026-08-23 on an isolated fixture: a target file whose fragment truly
sits at line 15, cited four ways, run under
`queue.py --store <dir> lint --strict stale-citation`.

| fragment | cited at | default window | `--citation-window 0` |
|---|---|---|---|
| copied with its 4-space indent | 9 | silent | silent |
| copied with its 4-space indent | 2 | silent | silent |
| same fragment, trimmed | 9 | silent | caught, "now at line 15" |
| same fragment, trimmed | 2 | caught | caught, "now at line 15" |
| same fragment, trimmed *(control)* | 15 | silent | silent |

Thirteen lines off and silent under the strictest gate available. The trimmed
row cited at 9 is the documented window behaviour and is working as designed;
the two indented rows are not reached by either setting. The last row is the
control that makes the silences mean something — a correct citation, where
silence is the right answer, which is what separates these readings from a
harness that never ran the check.

**The premise the design rests on is false.** The comment above `CITATION_RE`
says the fragment may not open with a space, "which `grep -n` never emits
either". `grep -n` emits the line's own text including its indentation —
measured on this repo:

```
$ grep -n 'rec = tip_is_recoverable(cwd, name)' plugins/branch-guard/hooks/branch-guard.py
1294:    rec = tip_is_recoverable(cwd, name)
```

So the store's own instruction — copy the whole citation from `grep -n` output —
walks an author into the unchecked case on any indented line, which is nearly
every line of Python, Go, or shell inside a function. That is the opposite of a
corner case: the correct-looking workflow produces the silent citation, and the
author has to know to hand-trim to get a checked one.

Found while filing Q120 during Q110: the first draft cited line 1443 where the
fragment sat at 1294, and passed both the default window and
`--citation-window 0`. Reading the line is what caught it.

This is Q115's subject — that row is about citation drift being under-gated and
the obvious tightening misfiring — and Q115 does not mention this failure mode.
Filed separately rather than appended so the finding lands with its own
measurement; whoever works either row should read the other. (Named in prose
deliberately: Q115 is live, and a link would dangle when it ships.)

Two candidate fixes, and the second is the one that needs deciding:

- **Accept a fragment that opens with whitespace**, comparing it stripped
  against a stripped line. Cheap, and it makes `--citation-window 0` mean what
  its help text says. The delimiter argument for `\S` is about where a fragment
  *ends*, which the trailing backtick already settles, so the leading-space rule
  is not carrying the weight the comment claims.
- **Or refuse it loudly** rather than degrading to a bare citation. A citation
  whose fragment cannot be delimited is currently read as bare and checked the
  old way, which the code documents as degrading quietly rather than crying
  wolf. That trade is defensible for a fragment nobody could parse and wrong
  here, where the author supplied a perfectly good fragment and got no check.

Either way the fixture above belongs in the suite, including its control — a
test that only asserts the indented case is caught would pass against a checker
that reports every citation as stale.
